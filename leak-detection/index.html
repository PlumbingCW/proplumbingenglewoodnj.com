Emergency Leak Detection in Englewood, NJ

Find the hidden leak behind the high water bill, the stained ceiling or the meter that will not stop, without opening walls at random.

Call (551) 249-3045

Leak detection in Englewood, NJ means finding where hidden water is escaping from a pipe, using testing and listening instead of guessing and cutting. If water is spraying or coming through a ceiling, close the main shutoff valve at once and call our 24/7 line. A slow leak behind a wall can usually wait for a same-day appointment when available.

Most leaks do not announce themselves. They show up as a water bill that jumped, a musty smell in a closet, paint that bubbles, or a floor that feels warm under bare feet. By the time a stain is visible, the water has often travelled along a joist or a pipe a long way from the source.

This page walks through the symptoms in the order people tend to notice them, then covers where leaks hide, from the attic to the buried line under the yard. When the fix goes beyond finding the leak, the next step is usually burst pipe repair or water service line repair.

Water you can see or hear right now

Spraying water, a ceiling that is sagging or dripping steadily, or water near an electrical panel all need the same first move. Close the main shutoff, which is usually where the water line enters the basement or utility room, near the meter. Then open the lowest faucet to drain the pipes and ease the pressure.

A sagging ceiling can drop without warning, so stay out from under it. If water is near outlets or the panel, do not touch anything electrical while standing in it, and call an electrician.

  • 1Shut the main valve and note whether it turns fully, since a stuck valve is its own repair
  • 2Move electronics, rugs and valuables out of the wet area
  • 3Place a bucket under active drips and move furniture out from under a stained ceiling
  • 4Photograph the damage for your insurer before any cleanup
  • 5Do not drain the water heater without turning off its power or gas control first, following the manufacturer's directions
  • 6Write down when you first noticed the problem and what you did

Meter still running with every tap off

Your water meter is the quickest leak detector in the house. Turn off every tap, the dishwasher, the washer and the icemaker, then look at the meter. A small dial or triangle that keeps turning means water is moving somewhere you cannot see. Many meters also have a low-flow indicator that spins even for a slow drip.

A toilet that leaks past its flapper is the most common culprit. Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank, wait 15 minutes without flushing, and look for color in the bowl. A worn flapper is a simple job under toilet repair. If the toilet is dry and the meter still moves, the leak is in a wall, under the floor or in the line between the meter and the house.

An unexplained spike on a water bill is worth a call even if the meter looks quiet when you check it, since some leaks run only when a pump or irrigation zone cycles. Ask your supplier for past readings, so the plumber can see whether use climbed slowly or jumped overnight.

Stained ceiling, damp wall or wet attic

Water follows gravity and framing, so the stain is rarely right under the leak. A stain below a bathroom may come from a shower pan, a supply line or a drain, and the plumber has to separate those. Pressure testing the supply, running each fixture in turn, and scanning with a moisture meter and thermal camera usually narrows it down.

Not every ceiling stain is plumbing. Roof leaks, condensation on cold ductwork and ice dams can leave the same brown ring. If it appears only after rain, start with a roofer. If it shows up on a dry day or keeps growing, the plumbing is the first suspect.

In the attic, look for damp insulation, rusty nails or a trail of staining on the joists. Stay on the walkway boards and off the drywall between joists, since your foot can go through the ceiling below.

Under the house and in the crawlspace

Many New Jersey homes have a basement, but some additions, porches and older ranch houses sit over a crawlspace. Supply and drain lines run under the floor there, where small leaks drip for months unnoticed. Signs include a musty smell, sagging insulation, soft spots in the floor and rising humidity.

Wear gloves and a mask if you look, because wet crawlspaces can hold mold, droppings and sharp debris. Stay out if the space is flooded, and never go in with standing water near wiring. A plumber can inspect with a flashlight and moisture meter and test each supply line in turn to see which one loses pressure.

Buried lines, sprinklers and the meter box

The service line runs from the meter or street connection to the house, buried below the frost line. A leak there can surface as a soggy patch, unusually green grass in dry weather or a drop in pressure. Plumbers narrow the spot with acoustic listening equipment, pipe tracing and a pressure test that isolates the line from the house.

Sprinkler systems are a common hidden leak. A broken head, a cracked valve or a stuck zone valve can run water for weeks. Turn the system off at the controller and check the meter again. If it still moves with irrigation off, the problem is back in the house or the service line.

The meter box at the curb or sidewalk can fill with water if a fitting at the meter is leaking, and the supplier usually owns the meter. If you see water in the box, call the supplier named on your bill. Who owns which section of pipe varies, so ask where their side ends and yours begins.

Pool and spa leaks are a separate job. A bucket test helps separate evaporation from a leak, and the plumbing to the equipment pad can be pressure tested, but shell and liner leaks belong to a pool contractor.

Warm floors and the slab leak question

Slab leaks get a lot of attention, but New Jersey homes more often have basements than slabs, and supply lines usually run in basement ceilings where a leak can be seen. That changes with an addition, a garage, a sunroom or a ground-level unit built on a concrete slab, and those do exist around Bergen County.

On a slab, the signs are a warm spot on the floor, the sound of running water with fixtures off and a damp patch under flooring. Confirming it takes listening equipment and pressure testing. Repair choices include rerouting the line through walls or the ceiling, or breaking the slab at the leak, and the right one depends on what the line serves and what sits above it.

What affects the price of leak detection

Finding a leak and fixing it are separate costs, and these factors drive the first one.

Number of areas to test

A single wall is quicker than a whole house with several suspect zones.

Access to the pipes

Open basement ceilings are quick to inspect. Finished ceilings, tiled floors and tight crawlspaces take more time and care.

Equipment and method

Pressure testing, acoustic listening, thermal imaging and tracer gas each suit different leaks, and the method affects time on site.

Depth and length of buried lines

A long service line under pavement or landscaping is harder to trace than a short exposed one.

Repair after detection

Opening a wall, patching drywall or digging a trench changes the total well beyond the search itself.

Sudden accidental discharge may be covered, and some policies help pay to open walls to reach a leak. Gradual leaks, wear and maintenance are usually not covered, so ask your carrier before work starts.
