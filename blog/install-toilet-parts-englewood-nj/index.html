How to Install Toilet Parts in Englewood, NJ: Fill Valve, Flapper, Wax Ring, and More

Most toilet parts are safe for a careful homeowner to replace.

Call (551) 249-3045
By the Englewood Pro Plumbing teamPublished October 30, 2026Updated October 30, 20266 min read

Most toilet parts are safe for a careful homeowner to replace. A fill valve, a flapper, and a supply line are all inside the tank or behind the toilet and take about half an hour each. A wax ring and closet bolts mean lifting the whole toilet, which is heavier and messier.

In Englewood, NJ, the real dividing line is what you see under the toilet. A rocking bowl, a soft floor, or a rusted flange is not a parts problem. That is where a licensed plumber should take over.

Which part fixes which problem

Match the symptom to the part before you shop.

SymptomLikely partDIY level
Tank keeps refilling or hissesFill valve or flapperEasy
Toilet runs on its own for a few secondsFlapper or flapper seatEasy
Water on floor near the wallSupply line or stop valveModerate
Water at the base of the toiletWax ring, closet bolts, or flangeHarder
Rocking toiletLoose bolts or a damaged flangeCheck first, then decide
Water drips from tank boltsTank-to-bowl gasket or bolt washersModerate

A flapper that leaks into the bowl is the most common culprit behind a toilet that runs.

Replacing the fill valve

  1. 01

    Shut off water and empty the tank

    Close the stop valve behind the toilet, flush, and sponge out the last water.

  2. 02

    Disconnect the supply line

    Loosen the nut under the tank and keep a towel handy for drips.

  3. 03

    Remove the old valve

    Unscrew the locknut under the tank and lift the valve out.

  4. 04

    Set the new valve at the right height

    Adjust it per the package so the top sits above the overflow tube.

  5. 05

    Reconnect and set the water level

    Open the stop slowly and adjust the float so the water stops about an inch below the top of the overflow tube.

The flapper and the chain

The flapper is the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank that lifts when you flush. A worn flapper warps and no longer seals, so tank water leaks into the bowl and the fill valve refills it. Replacement flappers snap onto pegs or clip onto the overflow tube.

Match the size. Many toilets use a 2-inch flapper, and newer ones often use 3-inch. A mismatch leaks. The chain should have just a little slack. Too tight and the flapper stays up, too loose and it won't lift.

If a new flapper still leaks, run a fingernail around the flapper seat. Mineral buildup or a nick there stops a good seal. A light cleaning may help, and a rough seat is a reason to call.

Supply line and stop valve

A braided stainless supply line, commonly 3/8 inch at the stop valve, connects the valve to the fill valve. Replace it if it's bulging, rusted, or kinked. Tighten by hand plus a quarter turn.

Old multi-turn stop valves can fail when you turn them. They may not shut off fully, or the stem may start to drip. That is a sign the valve needs replacing, which means shutting off the main and doing more plumbing work than a parts swap.

Do not force a stuck valve. A cracked valve behind a wall can flood a bathroom, and that is a fast way to turn a ten-minute job into an emergency.

Setting a toilet on a new wax ring

  1. 01

    Shut off, drain, and disconnect

    Empty the tank and bowl, disconnect the supply, and remove the nuts on the closet bolts.

  2. 02

    Lift straight up

    A toilet can weigh 60 to 100 pounds or more. Get a second person, and set it on a towel.

  3. 03

    Scrape and inspect

    Remove the old wax, then look at the flange for cracks, rust, or a poor fit to the floor.

  4. 04

    Install new closet bolts and ring

    Slide new bolts into the flange slots and press on a new wax ring or a rubber seal.

  5. 05

    Lower, tighten gradually, and test

    Press the bowl down level, tighten the nuts alternately, and don't overtighten the porcelain. Fill the tank and check the base after several flushes.

What signals a flange or floor problem

Stop and call a plumber if you find any of these.

  • ✓A flange that sits below the finished floor or is cracked
  • ✓Rust or corrosion on a cast iron flange
  • ✓Soft, spongy, or discolored flooring around the base
  • ✓A toilet that keeps rocking after new bolts
  • ✓Sewer smell even after a new wax ring
  • ✓Repeated leaks at the base after a reset

When a parts swap is the wrong plan

A toilet that has leaked at the base for months may have rotted the subfloor. Fixing the wax ring does not fix a soft floor. The floor may need repair before the toilet sits on it again.

Water stains on a ceiling below, black mold near the base, or a spongy feel under your foot are warning signs. They point to a larger leak than a seal can explain.

That is when toilet repair and installation by a licensed plumber is the safer choice, and new flange piping falls under plumbing installation. They can check the flange, repair or replace it, and set the toilet to the finished floor.

What Englewood and Bergen County homeowners should know about toilet repairs

New Jersey requires plumbing to be performed by a master plumber, and licences expire every two years on June 30 of odd-numbered years. Ask any plumber who works on your flange or floor for the licence number and confirm it with the State Board of Examiners of Master Plumbers. Replacing small parts like a flapper in your own home is a different matter from hiring someone to cut into floors and pipes.
