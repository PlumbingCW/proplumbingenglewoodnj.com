Can You Install a Hot Water Heater Yourself in New Jersey?

In New Jersey, the honest answer is no, not legally.

Call (551) 249-3045
By the Englewood Pro Plumbing teamPublished October 30, 2026Updated October 30, 20267 min read

In New Jersey, the honest answer is no, not legally. The State Board of Examiners of Master Plumbers says plumbing work is to be done by a licensed master plumber, and a water heater swap is plumbing work.

The legal point is only part of it. A water heater touches gas or high-amperage electric power, a flue that carries exhaust out of the house, and a pressure vessel full of hot water. Homeowners in Englewood, NJ can safely do the maintenance around it, but the install itself should go to a licensed plumber.

What the New Jersey law says

New Jersey law says plumbing shall only be performed by a master plumber, along with authorized plumbing contractors and their employees. The rule comes from the State Board of Examiners of Master Plumbers, part of the Division of Consumer Affairs. It is there to protect the people who live in the house after the work is done.

Because of that rule, a homeowner who connects a water heater alone can run into trouble at several points. The town may not issue or sign off a permit to someone who is not licensed, an insurer may question a claim tied to unlicensed work, and a buyer's inspector may flag it at resale.

Permit rules vary by job, so ask the Englewood Building & Code Enforcement office what applies in your case. Owners in Tenafly or Englewood Cliffs should ask their own borough's building department.

Not every water heater job carries the same risk. This table separates what you can do from what should go to a licensed plumber.

The upper rows are fine to try. The rest are where installs go wrong and where the legal exposure sits.

The three hazards that make this more than a plumbing job

Gas is the first. A gas water heater has a supply line, a shutoff valve, and a burner that needs a clean, correctly sized flow of fuel. A poorly sealed connection can leak, and leaks are not always easy to smell. If you ever suspect a gas leak, leave the building and call the gas utility or 911 first.

Venting is the second. Combustion gases from a gas heater must leave through a flue pipe with the right slope, joints, and clearances. A flue that is disconnected or backdrafting can spill carbon monoxide into the home, which you cannot see or smell.

Pressure is the third. A tank heater stores water at household pressure and heat, and it relies on a temperature and pressure relief valve to protect it if the thermostat fails. A missing or blocked relief valve is a real hazard. Many installs also include an expansion tank so pressure from heated water does not strain the system.

What a proper installation involves

  1. 01

    Choose the right unit

    Match the tank size to the household and the fuel type to what the house already has. A plumber also checks the space, venting path, and clearances.

  2. 02

    Shut off and drain the old heater

    Water, gas, or power are turned off. The tank is drained to a floor drain or outside and disconnected.

  3. 03

    Set the new unit and connect the water

    Supply lines, shutoff valves, and the relief valve discharge pipe go in with code-appropriate materials. Dielectric or flex connectors are chosen for the pipe type.

  4. 04

    Connect fuel or power

    Gas connections get leak-tested, and electric units need a properly sized circuit hooked up by someone qualified.

  5. 05

    Vent, fill, and test

    The flue is attached and checked for draft. The tank fills with a hot tap open to release air, then the unit is lit and checked for leaks.

  6. 06

    Sign off

    Where a permit applies, the municipal inspector may need to see the finished work. Ask your plumber who handles that step.

What homeowners can do to extend a heater's life

These steps carry far less risk than an install, and they help the heater last.

  • ✓Lower the temperature setting to a level that is hot enough for you without excess scalding risk
  • ✓Lift the relief valve lever briefly and make sure water flows to the drain pipe, then let it snap shut
  • ✓Look for rust, moisture, or mineral crust on the fittings and base of the tank
  • ✓Flush a few gallons from the drain valve once a year to clear sediment
  • ✓Ask a plumber to check the anode rod if the heater is past about five years old
  • ✓Keep storage and clutter at least a few feet away from the heater and flue

Repair, replace, or install new

A heater that is leaking from the tank body is done. Water inside the tank rusts the steel, and that leak will not be fixed with sealant. Turn off the water supply and the gas or power, then call a plumber for water heater replacement before the leak damages the floor.

A heater that is only failing to heat, or that makes popping noises, may just need a part or a flush. If you are adding a heater where there has never been one, or changing fuel type, that is a new-fit job. A licensed plumber handles that as water heater installation, including venting and fuel runs.

Cost depends heavily on tank size, fuel, and venting changes. As a general industry ballpark, a straight tank swap commonly lands somewhere between $1,200 and $3,500, and moving fuel type or venting can push it higher. A clear price before work starts is the standard to ask for.

Hiring a plumber who is actually licensed

Ask the plumber for their New Jersey master plumber licence number, then confirm it with the State Board of Examiners of Master Plumbers. Licences are renewed every two years, expiring on June 30 of odd-numbered years, so ask whether the licence is current.

Ask whether the quote includes the permit if one applies, the old heater haul-away, and any new valves or an expansion tank. A written scope helps you compare two quotes fairly.

Under N.J.S.A. 13:32-3.3(a)3, plumbing work is to be performed by a master plumber, along with authorized plumbing contractors and their W-2 employees. Master plumber licences expire every two years on June 30 of odd-numbered years. That is a state rule, so it applies to a house in Englewood, in Tenafly, or in Englewood Cliffs.

The City of Englewood Building & Code Enforcement office is at 2-10 North Van Brunt Street and is open Monday to Friday, 9:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m., excluding holidays. The city's pages do not spell out plumbing permit rules, so ask the office before any work starts.
