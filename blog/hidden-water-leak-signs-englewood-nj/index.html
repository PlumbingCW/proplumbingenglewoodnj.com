Signs of a Hidden Water Leak in Your House: An Englewood, NJ Checklist

The most common signs of a hidden water leak are a water bill that creeps up, a meter that keeps moving when every fixture is off, damp or musty smells, stained walls or ceilings, and a floor spot that feels warm.

The most common signs of a hidden water leak are a water bill that creeps up, a meter that keeps moving when every fixture is off, damp or musty smells, stained walls or ceilings, and a floor spot that feels warm. Any one of these is worth a closer look.

In Englewood, NJ the quickest check is the meter test. Turn off every water use, read the meter, wait an hour or two, and read it again. If the numbers change, water is going somewhere. A toilet dye test finds the most common silent leak in a few minutes.

The meter test in five steps

  1. 01

    Stop all water use

    Turn off faucets, the dishwasher, washing machine, and ice maker. Pause any irrigation.

  2. 02

    Find the meter

    It is usually in the basement near the front wall, or in a curb box outside under a small lid.

  3. 03

    Read the dial

    Look for a small triangle or wheel that spins when water flows, and note the number on the register.

  4. 04

    Wait one to two hours

    Do not use any water, including flushing.

  5. 05

    Read it again

    A moving triangle or a changed number means a leak somewhere between the meter and a fixture.

What a bill that creeps up is telling you

A single high bill can come from a houseguest or a filled pool. A pattern of gradually rising use, with nothing changed at home, is different. Compare the last several bills with the same months from the previous year.

Slow leaks often hide inside that pattern. A running toilet or a pinhole in a pipe can add thousands of gallons per month without making a sound. The bill is often the first and only warning.

If the water supplier offers a way to see daily or hourly use, ask how to access it. Even a basic reading from the meter at night will tell you whether something is using water while everyone sleeps.

Mold, warped flooring, and swollen baseboards all follow long exposure to moisture. They are signs the leak has been active for a while.

How to check a toilet with dye

Toilets are the most common source of silent leaks. A worn flapper lets tank water seep into the bowl, and the fill valve refills the tank over and over. You may hear nothing at all.

Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank, put the lid back, and don't flush. Wait fifteen to twenty minutes. If color shows up in the bowl, the flapper or its seat is leaking. A new flapper is an inexpensive part and a reasonable DIY swap.

If the bowl stays clear but the meter still moves, the leak is elsewhere. Close the stop valve behind the toilet to see whether the meter stops, and then work through other fixtures one by one.

Quick checks you can do without tools

Work through these before assuming the worst.

  • ✓Look under every sink for drips, stains, or crust on the connections
  • ✓Check around the water heater for dampness or rust at the base
  • ✓Run a hand along basement walls and the ceiling beneath bathrooms
  • ✓Inspect the washing machine hoses for bulges and wetness
  • ✓Walk the yard between the street and the house for a soft or unusually green strip
  • ✓Listen near the meter for a hiss when everything is off

When to stop investigating and call

Call a licensed plumber if the meter moves with every fixture isolated, if a wall or ceiling is wet, or if you cannot trace the source in an hour. Hidden leaks do their damage while you debug them.

A leak detection visit uses tools like acoustic listening, thermal imaging, and moisture meters to find a leak without opening walls blindly. The goal is a small, targeted opening instead of a large demolition.

If the leak sits between the street and the house, the repair may involve the buried pipe. That is a job for water service line repair. Until the plumber arrives, close the main valve and keep electrical devices away from standing water.

What Englewood and Bergen County homeowners should know about hidden leaks

Englewood has 11,563 housing units in a city incorporated in 1899, so plumbing materials vary a lot from one street to the next. Each house has to be judged on its own pipes. Ask a plumber which materials they see in your supply lines and which are more prone to pinholes.

Much of northern Bergen County drinks water from Veolia's Oradell Reservoir system, so check the supplier named on your water bill, and ask that supplier for its annual water quality report. The same bill is where you find your meter details and whom to call about the meter itself. Before you dig up or cut anything near the meter, ask the supplier who owns which part of the line.

The same advice applies in Teaneck, which has about 41,246 residents, and in Tenafly, where East Hill rises sharply east of downtown. On a sloped lot a leak can surface downhill from the actual break, so the wet spot may not be where the pipe failed.

Check a plumber before you hire

New Jersey law says plumbing work must be done by a licensed master plumber. Ask for the licence number, then confirm it with the State Board of Examiners of Master Plumbers, part of the New Jersey Division of Consumer Affairs.

Permit rules vary by job, so ask the Englewood Building & Code Enforcement office before work begins.

Questions homeowners ask

A small continuous leak can waste many gallons a day without any visible sign. Over a month that adds up to thousands of gallons. The meter test is the fastest way to know whether it is happening in your home.

Coverage depends on the policy and on whether the damage was sudden and accidental or gradual. Long-term seepage and wear are often excluded. Ask your carrier before you start repairs, and take photos of the damage.

Warm spots, a sound of water, and a moving meter are clues, but pinpointing the exact location needs equipment. A plumber can narrow it down without breaking up the floor. Avoid cutting the slab until the leak is located.

Water follows slope and soil layers underground, so it may surface far from where the pipe broke. On a hillside lot, a wet patch downhill can come from a service line or house pipe higher up. A locator tool is more reliable than guessing.
