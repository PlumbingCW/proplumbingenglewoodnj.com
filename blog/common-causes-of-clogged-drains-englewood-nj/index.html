Common Causes of Clogged Drains, and When It Is the Main Line

The most common causes of clogged drains are hair and soap scum in bathrooms, grease and food scraps in kitchens, and flushed wipes in toilets.

Call (551) 249-3045
By the Englewood Pro Plumbing teamPublished October 30, 2026Updated October 30, 20268 min read

The most common causes of clogged drains are hair and soap scum in bathrooms, grease and food scraps in kitchens, and flushed wipes in toilets. Tree roots, a sagging section of pipe, and a blocked vent cause the harder cases.

The quickest way to sort it out is to count how many fixtures are affected. One slow drain is usually a local clog you can often fix. Several fixtures acting up at once points to the main drain, and that is a job for a licensed plumber in Englewood, NJ.

Match the symptom to the likely cause

Start with what you see and where. This table gives the usual suspect and the usual next step.

What you noticeLikely causeUsual next step
Shower or tub drains slowly, standing water around your anklesHair wrapped with soap scum near the drain or in the trapPull the stopper, clear by hand, or use a plastic drain tool
Kitchen sink slow, then backs up into the other basinGrease and food scraps coating the lineSnake the trap arm. Call a plumber if it returns fast
Toilet clogs often, plunger works only brieflyWipes, too much paper, or a partial blockage farther downPlunge properly, then have the line checked if it repeats
Gurgling sounds and slow drains in several roomsPartial main line clog or a blocked ventStop using water and call a licensed plumber
Basement floor drain bubbles when the washer drainsMain line restriction, often roots or a bellyCamera inspection of the line

Smell matters too. A sewer odor with slow drains usually means the blockage is past the fixture, not in it.

The everyday culprits at a single fixture

Hair is a leading cause of slow bathroom drains. It does not dissolve, so it catches on the rough spots of the pipe or on the stopper mechanism and then holds soap, toothpaste, and skin oils against it. A strainer in the shower costs a few dollars and prevents most of this.

Soap scum is a quieter cause. Bar soap contains fats that react with minerals in the water and form a pasty film on the pipe wall. The film narrows the opening, and hair and debris then land on it like they would on glue.

In kitchens, grease and food scraps are the standard cause. Fat cools and sticks, coffee grounds settle, and starchy food such as rice and pasta swells in the line. A garbage disposal grinds food finer, but fine particles still settle in the pipe and build on that grease coating.

Wipes and other things that never break down

Wipes labelled flushable still do not break apart the way toilet paper does. They hold their fibres together, snag on rough joints, and gather in a rope that other waste then clings to. This is a leading cause of toilet and main line stoppages.

Feminine products, paper towels, cotton swabs, dental floss, and cat litter belong in the trash for the same reason. Floss is a quiet offender because it wraps around other debris and pulls it into a mass.

A toilet has its own internal trap, and it is narrow. Too much paper at once can plug it, but a proper plunger usually clears that. If a toilet clogs repeatedly and nothing seems to be going in that should not be, the blockage may be downstream of the toilet.

Problems inside the pipe itself

Tree roots are attracted to the moisture and nutrients in a sewer line. They enter through tiny cracks or loose joints, then grow into thick mats that catch paper and waste. Roots are most common in older clay, cast iron, or concrete-type lines, and they return unless the pipe is repaired.

A belly is a low spot where a section of pipe has sagged, often because the soil beneath it shifted. Water and waste pool in the dip instead of flowing through, and solids settle there. A belly usually shows up as a line that clogs again after every cleaning, and a camera will show it.

Pipe can also collapse, corrode, or separate at a joint. Old cast iron flakes inside as it ages, and that rough interior narrows the opening and catches debris. These issues do not clear with a snake, because the problem is the pipe, not what is in it.

Blocked vents: the cause people overlook

Every drain needs air as well as slope. Vent pipes run up through the roof so air can enter behind the draining water. If a vent is blocked by leaves, a bird nest, or ice, the drain can gurgle, drain slowly, or pull water out of a trap and let sewer gas into the room.

A vent problem rarely looks like a normal clog. Look for gurgling from a drain nobody was using, slow drains on several fixtures with no clog found, and sewer odor near a trap that normally holds water. Checking a roof vent means working at height, so leave that to a licensed plumber.

How to tell a fixture clog from a main line clog

  1. 01

    Count the affected fixtures

    One slow sink points to a local clog. A tub, a toilet, and a sink all acting up point downstream.

  2. 02

    Check the lowest drain

    A basement floor drain or lowest-floor shower is the first place a main line backup shows.

  3. 03

    Run the washing machine

    If the basement drain gurgles or rises when the washer empties, the main line is restricted.

  4. 04

    Flush a toilet and watch the tub

    Bubbling or water rising in the tub after a flush is a classic main line sign.

  5. 05

    Locate the cleanout

    A capped pipe in the basement or yard gives a plumber direct access. Do not open one with water backing up behind it.

What to try yourself and what to leave alone

A flange plunger, a plastic hair tool, and a cleaned P-trap handle most single-fixture clogs. Put a bucket under the trap, unscrew the slip nuts by hand, and clear it out. These are safe, cheap, and often finish the job in ten minutes.

Skip the liquid drain openers if you can. They sit in a standing clog and can damage some pipe and fixtures, and they are a hazard to whoever opens the line next. Never mix two products, because the combination can release dangerous gas.

When more than one fixture is affected, stop adding water. Call a licensed plumber and ask for drain cleaning with a camera look, so the cause is identified instead of guessed. Homeowners in Leonia and Teaneck face the same sorting problem. If the cause is deep in the line, a plumber can arrange sewer line cleaning from a cleanout.

What Leonia's hills and Englewood's older housing mean for your drains

Leonia is a small borough with a surprising range of elevation. The western part sits as low as about 5 feet, the centre is about 115 feet, and the eastern part rises to about 318 feet. Drains run on gravity, so in a hilly area like this the lowest fixture in a house, often a basement floor drain, is usually the first place a main line backup shows.

Englewood is long settled, with passenger rail arriving in 1859, which suggests many older houses in the area. Older sewer laterals may be clay or cast iron, but each house has to be checked individually. A camera inspection is the honest way to learn what your line is made of and whether roots or a belly are the cause.

Check a plumber before you hire

New Jersey law says plumbing work must be done by a licensed master plumber. Ask for the licence number, then confirm it with the State Board of Examiners of Master Plumbers, part of the New Jersey Division of Consumer Affairs.

Permit rules vary by job, so ask the Englewood Building & Code Enforcement office before work begins.
