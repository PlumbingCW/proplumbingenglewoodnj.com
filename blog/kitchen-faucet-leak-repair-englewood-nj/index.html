Kitchen Faucet Leak Repair in Englewood, NJ: Where It Leaks and What to Do

Kitchen faucet leak repair starts with finding where the water actually comes from.

Call (551) 249-3045
By the Englewood Pro Plumbing teamPublished October 30, 2026Updated October 30, 20266 min read

Kitchen faucet leak repair starts with finding where the water actually comes from. A drip at the spout usually means a worn cartridge or washer. Water around the handle or the base of the spout points to worn O-rings. Wet cabinet floors often come from supply lines, shutoff valves, or the drain, not the faucet itself.

In Englewood, NJ, most of these are manageable for a careful homeowner if the shutoff valves work. Shut the water off first, match the replacement part to the exact faucet, and stop if anything corrodes or won't turn. A faucet that is old, pitted, or leaking from the body is usually cheaper to replace than to chase.

Where the water shows up and what it usually means

Dry the area with a towel, run the faucet, and watch where the first bead of water appears.

Where it leaksLikely causeTypical fix
Drip from the spout when the handle is offWorn cartridge, washer, or valve seatReplace the cartridge or washer and seat
Water around the handle baseWorn O-ring or loose packing nutReplace O-rings or snug the nut
Water at the base of a swivel spoutDried or cut spout O-ringsReplace the O-rings with matching ones
Wet under the sink, drips from the hose connectionsLoose or failing supply linesTighten gently or replace the lines
Wet under the sink, only when the sprayer is usedCracked sprayer hose or loose fittingReplace the hose or reseat the fitting
Water drips from the shutoff valve stemWorn valve packingReplace the valve, a licensed plumber task

If water appears from a crack in the metal body of the faucet, repair is rarely worth the effort.

Cartridge, washer, and disc faucets in plain words

Older compression faucets use rubber washers that press against a valve seat as you turn the handle. Over time the washer hardens and the seat roughens, and the faucet drips. These faucets usually have two handles.

Most single-handle faucets use a cartridge, a plastic or brass cylinder with rubber seals that moves with the handle. Others use a ceramic disc cartridge, which is very durable but can still leak if grit damages the discs. A few use a ball assembly with springs and seals.

The takeaway is that the part you need depends on the faucet type and brand. Take the old part to a supply store or read the model number off the faucet. A wrong cartridge can make a leak worse.

A safe order of operations before you touch anything

  1. 01

    Shut off the water

    Turn the small oval or round stop valves under the sink clockwise. If they won't move, shut off the main instead.

  2. 02

    Plug the drain and open the faucet

    A towel or stopper keeps small parts from falling into the drain, and opening the faucet releases pressure.

  3. 03

    Photograph each layer

    Take pictures as you remove the handle, cap, and retaining nut so reassembly is straightforward.

  4. 04

    Replace the failed part

    Swap in the exact cartridge, washer, or O-rings, and clean the valve seat with a cloth.

  5. 05

    Reassemble and test slowly

    Open the stops, run the water, and check every connection with a dry paper towel.

What a homeowner can safely do, and where to stop

Replacing a cartridge, O-rings, or a braided supply line is a reasonable first project. Tighten fittings by hand and then a quarter turn with a wrench. Over-tightening cracks plastic nuts and crushes rubber seals.

Stop when you meet corrosion that has fused parts together, a shutoff valve that won't close, or a nut that rounds off. Forcing them can snap a pipe inside the wall. A shutoff valve that drips when turned is also a sign that the valve should be replaced.

Never wrap tape or sealant around a leaking joint and call it done. A seep that is hidden under a cabinet floor can rot wood and grow mold for months. If you cannot find the source, a leak detection visit can trace where the water begins.

Signs it is time to replace rather than repair

A new faucet makes sense when several of these are true.

  • ✓The finish is peeling, pitted, or flaking at the base
  • ✓Parts for the model are no longer sold
  • ✓The faucet has been repaired more than once in a short time
  • ✓Water leaks from the body or the neck of the spout
  • ✓Low flow persists after cleaning the aerator
  • ✓The mounting hardware is loose or the sink deck is cracked

Why a small drip is worth fixing now

A faucet that drips once a second can waste thousands of gallons over a year. That affects the water bill, but the larger risk is the hidden damage. Water that runs down the back of the faucet or along the supply lines pools in the cabinet.

Particleboard cabinet floors swell quickly when they get wet. Ask yourself when the cabinet last looked dry and whether there is a stain or a musty smell. If there is, schedule a plumber who handles faucet repair and can check the connections underneath.

In the meantime, a small bowl under a slow drip is fine. A steady stream is not. Shut off the stop valve and call.
