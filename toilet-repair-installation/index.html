A toilet that will not stop running, rocks on the floor or spills over the rim is hard to ignore. Toilet repair in Englewood, NJ means finding the part that failed, such as the fill valve, flapper, wax ring, flange or supply line, and fixing or replacing it so the fixture seals and flushes again. Our line is open 24/7 when water is on the floor.

While you wait, shut the oval-handled supply valve on the wall behind the toilet by turning it clockwise. If the bowl is rising, lift the tank lid and press the flapper down. Lay towels around the base, keep everyone out of the bathroom, and skip further flushes until the cause is clear.

Quick checks before you call

A few minutes of looking can tell a plumber a lot over the phone and sometimes saves a visit.

  • ✓Water on the floor around the base after a flush, not before it
  • ✓A bowl that rocks or shifts when you sit down
  • ✓Tank water level sitting at or above the overflow tube
  • ✓A hissing or trickling sound minutes after the last flush
  • ✓A supply valve that is stuck, crusty or drips when turned
  • ✓Only this toilet slow, or the tub and sinks gurgling as well

Most toilet complaints trace back to a short list of parts. This table shows the common pairings.

A wet floor does not always mean a bad wax ring. Condensation on a cold tank can look the same, so a plumber checks before pulling the fixture.

Leaks at the base, wax rings and flanges

A wax ring is a thick gasket that seals the toilet outlet to the closet flange, the ring bolted to the floor above the drain. When the bowl is lifted for any reason, that ring should be replaced. Reusing it is a common reason a toilet leaks again after a quick repair.

If the flange is cracked, rusted or sitting below the finished floor after new tile went in, a fresh ring cannot seal well. A licensed plumber can fit a repair ring or replace the flange, then set the bowl level with new closet bolts. Slow base leaks soak the subfloor, so a damp or soft spot near the toilet deserves attention soon.

Running toilets, fill valves and supply valves

The fill valve refills the tank after each flush, and the flapper holds that water until you flush again. Either can wear out. A simple dye test helps. Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank, wait fifteen minutes without flushing, and look at the bowl. Color in the bowl points to the flapper.

Fill valves are cheap parts, and replacing one is usually quick. The harder question is whether the rest of the tank hardware is worn too. If the flush handle, chain and flapper are all stiff and brittle, replacing the full set often makes more sense than fixing one piece at a time.

The small valve on the wall, called an angle stop or supply valve, lets you shut the toilet off without closing the whole house. Older stops can seize, weep from the stem, or fail to close all the way. Forcing a stuck one can snap it.

If your valve will not turn, stop and call before the tank line fails. A plumber may need to close the main shutoff to swap the stop and the 3/8-inch braided supply line together. Both are inexpensive parts, but doing them as a pair avoids a second leak a month later.

Clogged toilet or a drain line problem?

One toilet that clogs once is usually a toilet problem. A plunger with a flange cup often clears it. Do not pour drain cleaner into the bowl, because the chemicals can splash back and may damage the fixture.

If the shower gurgles when you flush, or two fixtures slow down together, the blockage is probably in the branch line or main drain. That is a job for professional drain clearing, and a camera can show whether roots or a damaged section sit behind it. Repeat clogs in a single toilet can also mean a partly blocked vent or a poorly seated bowl.

When a toilet problem cannot wait

Call right away when water keeps flowing onto the floor, when sewage backs up into the bowl or tub, or when a supply line has failed and the valve will not close. Standing water can ruin a ceiling below, and sewage carries bacteria you should not touch.

If the bathroom floor is wet near an outlet or a light fixture, stay out and shut off that circuit at the panel only if you can reach it without standing in water.

  • 2Lift the tank lid and hold the flapper down if the bowl is rising
  • 3Move rugs and towels away, then soak up standing water to protect the floor
  • 4Keep children, pets and hands out of sewage water
  • 5Take photos of any damage before you start cleanup

Choosing and installing a new toilet

Sometimes the best repair is a new toilet. A cracked bowl, a tank that leaks through the porcelain, or a model that clogs weekly is worth replacing. Measure the rough-in, the distance from the wall to the center of the floor drain. Twelve inches is common, but older bathrooms vary.

Installation covers removing the old fixture, checking the flange, setting a new wax ring, leveling, bolting down, and connecting a new supply line. Adding a toilet in a new bathroom is bigger work, since it also involves drain and vent lines, as described under new plumbing fixture installation.

What affects the price of toilet repair

Toilet jobs range from a ten-minute part swap to a floor repair, so the price depends on what the plumber finds.

The failed part

A flapper or fill valve is a small repair. A flange or valve replacement takes more time and materials.

Pulling the toilet

Wax ring and flange work means lifting the bowl, cleaning the flange, and resetting it, which adds labor.

Floor or subfloor damage

A long-running leak can soften wood under the fixture and may need repair before the toilet goes back.

Fixture choice

A replacement toilet varies by bowl shape, flush design and who supplies it.

Access and timing

Tight bathrooms, stuck valves and after-hours calls change how long the job takes.

Homeowners policies may cover sudden accidental water discharge, such as a supply line that bursts. Gradual leaks, wear and routine maintenance are usually not covered, so ask your carrier before filing.
